The most-moved market on any prediction-market dashboard is usually the one you should trust least. A 40-cent move in a day looks like information — someone knows something. But in prediction markets, the biggest 24-hour movers are often where the least reliable prices live, and there's a small but real research base on why. This matters if you use a mover list like the one on the Polymarket Scanner (https://builtbytobias.com/polymarket-scanner/), which sorts markets by "Biggest mover (24h)" — here's what that sort is actually showing you.

A move is not the same thing as information arriving. In prediction markets, momentum is driven almost entirely by information arrival — a poll drops, a court ruling lands, an injury report hits — but the biggest moves are also where overreaction lives. A practitioner analysis of Kalshi and Polymarket puts it bluntly: "One favorable poll for a candidate that moves a contract 8 points in an hour, when the polling average has barely budged, is a textbook overshoot. The market is pricing the headline, not the aggregate" (PillarLab, 2026). The price moved; the underlying odds barely did. That gap is the whole story of why mover lists mislead.

Thin books make big moves cheap. The same analysis makes the volume point directly: "A jump from 30c to 50c on 40 contracts of volume is not the same signal as a jump from 30c to 50c on 4,000 contracts." And on Polymarket specifically, "a handful of large wallets pushing a book that's ten deep can look like a genuine trend when it's really one entity front-running its own thesis" (PillarLab, 2026). A mover with no volume behind it is closer to one person's order than to a crowd's re-pricing.

Even "efficient" markets underreact on impact — then drift. A 2026 study of live NBA event contracts on Kalshi (Angelini & De Angelis, University of Bologna) found prices move in the right direction when public information arrives, but only about 0.64-for-one relative to a benchmark probability model: "the missing adjustment predicts future price drift over the following several minutes." The underreaction is concentrated exactly where you'd expect: "Salient public signals are incorporated relatively quickly in liquid markets, but the same signals generate substantially greater underreaction when liquidity is low." Their summary is the cleanest version: markets "can aggregate public information quickly without necessarily incorporating it fully on impact."

So a big move can be both things at once — an overreaction to a headline and an incomplete response to the durable part of the news, with the price still drifting after. That's why the biggest mover is the worst place to read the market's true belief.

How to read a mover, then: (1) check the volume behind the move — 40 contracts is a whisper, 4,000 is a signal; (2) check the book's depth before the move, not just the new price; (3) ask about the catalyst's half-life — a court decision or confirmed injury is durable, an unconfirmed rumor or single outlier poll is "prime reversion within 24–72 hours" (PillarLab, 2026); (4) sanity-check against base rates — if the price swung 20 points and nothing fundamental changed, the pre-move level is the better anchor.

The moves are real; the interpretation is on you.

Honest uncertainty. Prediction-market microstructure research is young. The arXiv study covers one exchange (Kalshi) and one sport (NBA event contracts); PillarLab is a practitioner blog, not peer-reviewed. Treat the mechanisms as well-grounded and the specific numbers as directional, not gospel.
