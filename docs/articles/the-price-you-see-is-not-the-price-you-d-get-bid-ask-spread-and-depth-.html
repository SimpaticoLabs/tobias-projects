A market shows "63%" — but the cheapest YES share you can actually buy right now might be 66¢, and the cheapest you could sell might be 60¢. The number you're reading isn't a price at all; it's the midpoint between two prices that may be far apart.

Polymarket's own documentation is explicit about this: the price and probability displayed on a market is the midpoint of the bid-ask spread in the order book — and when that spread is wider than $0.10, the platform stops showing the midpoint entirely and shows the last traded price instead [¹](#source-1)[²](#source-2). That single rule is a quiet admission that a displayed number and an executable price are different things, and the gap between them is a real, measurable cost.

What the spread is, and why it costs you.

Every prediction-market contract trades between $0.00 and $1.00, and the price reflects the crowd's implied probability of the event happening [²](#source-2). The bid-ask spread is the gap between the highest price a buyer is offering (bid) and the lowest price a seller will accept (ask). Because that price range is so narrow compared to stocks or commodities, a small absolute spread carries a lot of weight: a 5-cent spread on a contract priced at $0.50 is 10% of the contract's value [²](#source-2). The spread is, in effect, the fee you pay for trading immediately rather than waiting for a better price — it compensates the market makers who quote both sides of the book for the risk of doing so [²](#source-2).

Spreads cluster into recognizable tiers that track liquidity. Based on observed behavior across Polymarket and Kalshi, roughly: 1–2 cents for high-liquidity markets with active market makers (major sports, flagship politics); 3–5 cents for moderate liquidity (individual Fed decisions, secondary sports); 6–10 cents for lower-liquidity niche events; and 10+ cents for illiquid or stale markets — the threshold where Polymarket itself gives up on the midpoint and shows last-traded price [²](#source-2). A February 2026 FalconX analysis found that spreads on Polymarket generally narrowed as contracts approached resolution, and that spreads across the full market set were wider than on the most heavily traded contracts [²](#source-2).

Depth is the second half — and it's the part people miss.

A tight spread is not the same thing as being able to trade. The best bid and ask are just the top level of the order book; "depth" is how many shares sit behind them. A market can look liquid at the touch and still not have enough size to absorb your order without moving the price against you. In a worked example, buying 100 shares against a book where 30 shares sit at 52¢, 70 at 54¢, and 200 at 57¢ means your average fill is 53.4¢ — not the 52¢ best ask, and certainly not the displayed midpoint [³](#source-3). Multiplying your whole order by the best ask would understate the cost by $1.40 in that single static example [³](#source-3).

This is why "volume" and "spread" both fail as lone proxies for tradability. Volume can be high while current depth is shallow if trading happened in bursts rather than through continuous quoting; a narrow spread with very little quantity behind it does not establish that a large order can fill near the display [²](#source-2)[³](#source-3). A displayed price, a depth estimate, an accepted order, and a settled position are all separate pieces of evidence [³](#source-3).

What this means when you're actually looking at markets.

The honest takeaway: the headline probability on any prediction market is a starting point, not a quote. Before you treat a displayed number as what you'd pay, you want two things — how wide the spread is (the immediate cost of crossing it), and how much depth sits behind the best prices (whether your order would move the market). A market showing a tight spread and a market showing a wide one are telling you different things about how much friction sits between the crowd's belief and your trade.

That's exactly the distinction the Polymarket Scanner is built to surface. Its sort options include Spread (tightest) — rank markets by how narrow their bid-ask spread is — and Depth ±2¢, which shows how much liquidity sits within two cents of the current price. Combined with the total-volume and liquidity sorts, that's a way to separate the markets where the displayed number is close to what you'd actually trade at from the ones where it isn't. The scanner is read-only and free, pulling live data from Polymarket's public Gamma API [⁴](#source-4); it won't tell you which markets to trade, but it will show you, at a glance, which ones have a real two-sided book behind that headline number.
