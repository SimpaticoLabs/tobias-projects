When Biden dropped out of the 2024 race, trading in Polymarket's presidential market surged — and Trump's price barely moved.

That's the opposite of the usual intuition. Most people read a prediction market's price as the crowd's belief and its volume as how strongly the crowd holds that belief: big trading should mean big repricing. But a study of Polymarket's complete on-chain ledger across the three biggest shocks of the 2024 election — the Biden–Trump debate, the assassination attempt on Trump, and Biden's withdrawal — found the two come apart. The same Trump market produced a price move that reversed, a move that stuck, and a burst of heavy trading with almost no price change at all (Tsang & Yang, 2026).

The pattern the authors find: price moves follow what the news actually revealed and how much of it was already priced in — not how much trading happened. The assassination attempt repriced Trump persistently, because it was genuinely new information about the Trump-versus-Democrat contest. The debate's bump largely reversed. And Biden's withdrawal saw the heaviest trading of the three with little Trump-price movement — the Democratic-nominee market had already priced a 70% probability of a Biden replacement before the announcement, so the news was mostly anticipated (Tsang & Yang, 2026). Volume, the authors conclude, is evidence of traders showing up; belief revision has to be read from linked outcome prices and the surprise in the news itself.

The second thing the research shows: moves can be too small before they're too big. In a study of live NBA game markets on Kalshi, prices responded to every public signal (a made shot, a lead change) in the right direction — but only about 0.64-for-one relative to the probability implied by the game state. The missing adjustment showed up as predictable price drift over the following minutes, and the underreaction was substantially worse when liquidity was low (Angelini & De Angelis, 2026). Markets aggregate public information quickly, the authors write, "without necessarily incorporating it fully on impact." (That study is sports markets, not politics — but the mechanism it isolates is liquidity, and liquidity is the same lever on Polymarket.)

And in genuinely thin markets, a single wallet can move the price on its own: Rothschild & Sethi (2016), studying a 2012 presidential prediction market, present evidence suggestive of manipulation by one large trader.

So a big 24-hour move is a question, not an answer. Before you trust one, ask three things:

  1. Did it move on real volume? A 20-point jump on $2,000 of trading in a thin market means something different from the same jump on $2 million.
  2. Was the news already priced? If a market had months to anticipate an event, the move after it lands can be small even when the event matters — the withdrawal case above.
  3. Did it reallocate across linked outcomes? Beliefs can shift between related markets (Biden YES → Harris YES) while one headline price stays flat.

That's the reading the Polymarket Scanner's Market Movers page is built for: it shows which markets shifted most in the last 24–48 hours — the "what moved" half of the story. The research above is the other half: what a move does and doesn't tell you. A market that jumped 20 points on thin volume and a market that jumped 20 points on a genuine, unanticipated surprise are different animals — and the scanner's sort-by-liquidity view is how you start telling them apart.
