The busiest market on a prediction-market board is usually the one nobody can agree on. That sounds backwards until you look at what actually drives trading volume: disagreement, not confidence.

Sort by 24-hour volume and you might assume the top of the list is where the crowd is most confident: the hottest, most-settled questions, the ones everyone is piling into because they're sure of the answer. The economics of trading say the opposite. A market trades heavily precisely because people don't agree, and the more contested the question, the more money changes hands. The busiest markets on the board are usually the most uncertain, not the most settled.

Why disagreement creates volume

The classic models of what drives trading volume start from a puzzle: if everyone saw the same information and interpreted it the same way, there'd be almost nothing to trade. You'd only trade to rebalance a portfolio or turn cash into a position, and you'd have no reason to buy while someone else sells, because you'd both value the asset the same.

The resolution, formalized by Milton Harris and Artur Raviv in their 1993 paper Differences of Opinion Make a Horse Race (Review of Financial Studies), is that traders who share the same information can still disagree about what it means. When two people look at the same facts and reach different conclusions about the odds, the confident one wants to buy and the other wants to sell, and that disagreement is the trade. In their model, trading volume is driven directly by differences of opinion, and it's why heavy volume and big price moves tend to arrive together.

Harrison Hong and Jeremy Stein made the same point for a broader audience in Disagreement and the Stock Market (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2007): the joint behavior of prices and volume is the signature that disagreement models explain, and that models assuming everyone agrees simply cannot. If traders only ever agreed, there'd be no reason for the volume we actually observe.

The intuition is simple enough to state without the math. When an outcome is predictable, there is little to trade: everyone already agrees, so there's no counterparty. When an outcome is genuinely uncertain and people read it differently, the market becomes active. As the LSE Business Review put it in 2026, the economics behind prediction markets' growth is "deceptively simple": when outcomes are uncertain, and people disagree, markets become active.

What that means when you're reading the board

So when the Polymarket Scanner's default sort, "Hot (24h volume)", puts a market at the top, that's not a signal of certainty. It's a signal of contestedness: a question people are actively fighting over, whether it's a close election, a disputed event, or a market where the outcome is genuinely up in the air. High volume means people are buying and selling against each other, not that they've converged on an answer.

That flips how you should read a busy market. A huge 24-hour volume figure is often read as "the crowd has weighed in and this is settled." The economics say the opposite: the market is busy because it isn't settled. The disagreement that produces the volume is the same reason the price can keep moving: there are plenty of people on the other side of every trade, ready to push back.

This matters for how you use a scanner at all. If you're looking for a confident, stable read, a quiet market with thin volume is not the trustworthy one either; thin books move on a single trade and their prices are noisy. The honest way to use volume is as what it is: a measure of how contested a question is, not how certain the crowd is. Sort by volume to find what people are arguing about; don't mistake it for what they've decided.
