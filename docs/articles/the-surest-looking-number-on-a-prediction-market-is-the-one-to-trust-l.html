A market that says something has a 3% chance is systematically too expensive — and a market that says 97% is systematically too cheap. On Polymarket, buying contracts priced under 10 cents lost 19.3 cents per dollar on average, while contracts at or above 90 cents earned money. The most extreme probabilities on the board are the least trustworthy numbers on it.

The bias is older than prediction markets.

The favorite–longshot bias was first documented in horse-race betting in 1949: bettors systematically overpay for unlikely outcomes (longshots) and underpay for likely ones (favorites) [¹](#source-1)[²](#source-2). It's one of the most persistent return patterns in betting markets ever since [¹](#source-1). What's new is that modern prediction markets let researchers measure it on a scale no racetrack ever offered: a study of Polymarket covering 588 million trades by 2.48 million accounts from November 2022 through March 2026 [¹](#source-1).

The numbers, from three independent analyses.

  • Dollar-weighted purchases below 10 cents lost 19.3 cents per dollar; purchases at or above 90 cents earned 0.83 cents (Cardozo & Rivero-Wildemauwe, 2026, on the full Polymarket ledger) [¹](#source-1).
  • At the extreme cheap end, contracts priced 2–5 cents lost about 21% per dollar on Polymarket (29% on Kalshi) at the mid, before fees (PDiTO, an open calibration study of 18,228 Polymarket contracts) [³](#source-3).
  • An independent analysis of almost three million resolved markets found a −39% average return on contracts bought at 2 to 5 cents (Koroleva, 2026) [²](#source-2).

How good are the prices in the middle of the range by comparison? A week before the close, markets with real trading are off by under two percentage points on average, and a day before the close they remove 82% of the error of a forecaster who only knows the base rate [²](#source-2). The residual mispricing "sits at the tails" — cheap longshots a little too dear, near-certain favorites a little too cheap [²](#source-2). In calibration terms, the logistic slope of 1.05 means exactly that: a slope above 1 = cheap contracts slightly overpriced, expensive ones slightly underpriced [³](#source-3).

Honest uncertainty: this is real, but it's not a money machine.

The same studies are careful about how strong the effect is. The 2–5 cent loss estimates carry wide confidence intervals [³](#source-3); the calibration slope excludes 1 at the 95% level but not at the stricter 99.5% level, which the authors describe as "suggestive rather than settled" [³](#source-3). And the magnitude depends on how you group the data: longshots lose 6.3 cents per dollar when each contract is weighted equally, but gain 4.1 cents when related contracts are grouped by parent event — and sports is a clear exception where longshots earn positive returns [¹](#source-1). The bias is a statistical tendency across hundreds of thousands of trades, not a rule about any single market.

Why does it exist at all? The two classic explanations are misperception (people systematically overestimate how likely unlikely events are) and lottery-ticket preference (people pay for the chance of a big payout, skewness love) [¹](#source-1)[²](#source-2). The Polymarket data narrows the story: longshot losses persist across experience levels and purchase methods, and the habitual longshot buyers don't lose disproportionately to anyone else — so it isn't simply naive bettors being fleeced [¹](#source-1).

What this means when you're reading a market.

Two practical takeaways. First, the tails are where calibration breaks: treat a 95%+ "sure thing" as slightly underpriced and a <5% longshot as slightly overpriced — the exact opposite of how the numbers feel. Second, thin markets lean toward YES: where the only available price is a stale last trade, it's often someone buying at a wide ask, so an extreme probability in a thin market is doubly suspect [³](#source-3).

That's where the Polymarket Scanner actually helps: its Highest probability and Lowest probability sorts surface exactly the extreme tails — the numbers this article says to read with the most skepticism. The scanner also shows each market's volume and liquidity in the same row, so you can tell whether an extreme price sits in a real two-sided market (where the bias is small) or a thin one (where the price itself may be noise).
