The traders most likely to be right about a prediction market deliberately wait to trade — which means a brand-new market's opening price is set by the people who know the least.

The problem with a market that's an hour old. When you open a prediction market created this morning, the number you see — "Yes" at 62¢ — looks like a probability: a crowd's best guess, distilled into a price. But there is no crowd yet. Someone has to be first. The opening price is the opinion of whoever happened to be looking at the market when it appeared: a market maker seeding both sides of the book, or a single curious trader who skimmed the same news you did. On Polymarket, market creators can list a market and the first trades are often just the creator and a bot or two. That number is a starting point, not a signal.

The people with the best information stay out on purpose. In an empirical study of price discovery in prediction markets, Wah, Lahaie and Pennock found that informed traders "are incentivized to trade as late as possible" and "avoid trading on their first entry into the market by weighing the market quote more heavily than their own information" [1]. Early in a market's life there is no information edge to exploit — the event is far away and the true probability hasn't moved yet — so the smart money waits. The early price is what's left over: uninformed order flow and maker quotes.

A prediction market's accuracy isn't a crowd phenomenon — it's a few people. Yale SOM's Theis Jensen and co-authors tested the "wisdom of crowds" explanation against real Polymarket trading data and found that accuracy "derives from the bets of a handful of skilled traders — who, unfortunately for the overwhelming majority of participants, also reap the lion's share of the financial winnings" [2]. The crowd isn't wrong; it's just not doing the heavy lifting. And those few skilled traders are exactly the ones who wait (see above). So a young market has neither the crowd nor the informed minority — it has whoever was first.

Young markets are also the most fragile to a single trade. Thin markets can move on "low-volume trades or order flow noise rather than information-motivated trading," and a single large position can move prices substantially "without reflecting any new fact" [3]. The Anatomy of Polymarket study of the 2024 election market quantifies the effect: in the platform's early, thin months, price impact was elevated, and as trading volume grew, Kyle's λ — the standard measure of how much a trade moves the price — "declined by more than an order of magnitude" [4]. Thin is not the same as wrong; it means one trade moves the number.

What to do with a young market. Treat the number as a hypothesis, not a fact, and check the two things that tell you how much weight to put on it: depth and spread. A wide bid-ask spread or shallow order book means the price carries a big error bar — the same market can look ten points different after the first few real trades. This is exactly what the Polymarket Scanner's "Newest first" sort and "New (≤7d)" filter are for: they isolate the youngest, least-informed markets on the site, and the "Depth ±2¢" and "Spread (tightest)" columns put the trust question right next to the number [5]. Sort by newest, then look at depth before you believe anything.

None of this means new markets are wrong — some open with professional market makers quoting both sides, and a young market can turn out to be right. It means the number carries less information than it looks like it does.
