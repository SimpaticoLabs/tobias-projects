Ivan Shishkin: The Man Who Spent His Life Painting Every Tree in Russia

In an era when photographers were beginning to capture the world with mechanical precision, one Russian painter set out to do something even more obsessive: paint every pine needle, every oak leaf, every blade of rye grass by hand — with a fidelity that would make a botanist nod in approval. His name was Ivan Shishkin, and he earned the nickname "Czar of the Forest" not through hyperbole, but through sheer, relentless dedication to trees.

The Boy Who Drew by Candlelight

Portrait of Shishkin by Ivan Kramskoi (1873). Public domain, Wikimedia Commons.

Ivan Ivanovich Shishkin was born on January 25, 1832, in the small town of Yelabuga, deep in Russia's Vyatka Governorate (now Tatarstan). His family were merchants — grain traders, practical people — and they had a clear plan for their son: graduate from the prestigious First Kazan Gymnasium and join the family business. [1]

Shishkin had other ideas. At 12, he enrolled in the gymnasium, but after five years he returned home. He was already drawing obsessively. His parents dismissed it as a phase. So Shishkin drew alone at night, by candlelight, teaching himself the craft his family refused to take seriously. [2] [3]

Eventually, his parents relented. In 1852, at age 20, Shishkin entered the Moscow School of Painting, Sculpture and Architecture. Four years later, he transferred to the Imperial Academy of Arts in St. Petersburg, where his talent became unmistakable. He won two small silver medals in his first year, a large silver medal in 1858 for Pine on Valaam, and the Grand Gold Medal in 1860 for View of Valaam Island — the Academy's highest honor, which came with a scholarship to study abroad. [1] [4]

The Making of a Forest Obsessive

Shishkin's training took him across Europe: Munich (where he studied animal painting under Benno Adam and Franz Adam), Zurich (under Rudolf Koller), Geneva, and Düsseldorf. [1] It was Düsseldorf that most shaped him — the school's commitment to meticulous naturalism and fidelity to detail aligned perfectly with his own instincts. His painting View in the Vicinity of Düsseldorf earned him an academician title from the Imperial Academy. [4]

But Shishkin was homesick. He returned to St. Petersburg in 1866, before his scholarship term ended, and never left Russia again for any extended period. [1]

Back home, he threw himself into the Russian landscape. He joined the Peredvizhniki ("The Wanderers" or "Itinerants") — a group of realist artists who rejected the Academy's rigid, formalist approach and sought to bring art to the public through traveling exhibitions. The group included legends like Ilya Repin, Isaac Levitan, and Alexei Savrasov. Their ethos: paint real life, honestly, accessibly. [2] [1]

For Shishkin, "real life" meant forests.

"Russia — country of landscapes… I hope the time will come, when all Russian nature, alive and penetrated by spirit, will look from canvases of Russian artists…" — Ivan Shishkin [2]

The Trees That Made Him Famous

Morning in a Pine Forest (1889). Shishkin painted the forest; Konstantin Savitsky added the bears. Public domain, Wikimedia Commons.

Several of Shishkin's paintings became cultural landmarks in Russia:

Rye (1878) — A vast golden field of ripening rye, stretching to the horizon under a heavy summer sky. A dirt road cuts through, flanked by towering pines. Two tiny figures walk in the distance, almost lost in the sea of grain. Shishkin called such land "expanse, space, land… God's grace… Russian wealth." The painting captures not just a view but a feeling: the particular stillness of late summer, the weight of an approaching storm on the horizon. [5]

Morning in a Pine Forest (1889) — Shishkin's most famous work. A misty pine forest at dawn, soft light filtering through fog and branches. The scene is silent, ancient, hushed. Morning in a Pine Forest became so iconic in Russia that it eventually appeared on candy wrappers (the "Mishka Kosolapy" — "Clumsy Bear" — chocolate, still sold today). [2] [5]

One interesting detail: the four bears playing on the fallen tree were painted not by Shishkin, but by his friend Konstantin Savitsky. At the collector Pavel Tretyakov's request, Savitsky's signature was removed, and the work has been historically credited solely to Shishkin. [2] [5]

Mast-Tree Grove (1898) — Shishkin's final painting. It depicts a grove of towering, straight-trunked pines — the kind prized as ship masts. The painting was exhibited at the Imperial Russian Museum's grand opening in March 1898. Shishkin was too ill to attend. He died the morning after the opening, at his easel, in the presence of a student. He was 66. [2]

What Made His Technique Different

Shishkin's paintings feel dense with information — and that's because they are. He approached each tree the way a portraitist approaches a face: as an individual with its own character, texture, and story. His drawings, especially, are studies in obsessive observation. [2]

But here's the paradox: his early work was so meticulously detailed that it looked plastic — hyperreal but lifeless. Compare his View of Valaam Island (1858) with his later Off the Coast of the Gulf of Finland (1889). The early painting is technically impressive but stiff. The later work breathes. He had learned that realism isn't about copying every detail — it's about knowing which details to emphasize and which to subordinate. [2]

His use of green became legendary. The "Czar of the Forest" could paint foliage in a way few artists could manage without it looking jarring. In Pine Trees Illuminated by the Sun (1886), the forest floor is a mosaic of greens, yellows, and reds — each brushstroke alive with color variance, yet the overall effect is one of effortless naturalism. [2]

His compositions frequently use what painters call "S-curves" — rivers, paths, and clearings that wind through the frame, drawing the viewer deeper into the scene. Misty Morning (1897) shows a river twisting through a foggy landscape; Rye uses the dirt road the same way. [2]

The Darkness Between the Trees

Shishkin's life contained deep tragedy. In 1874, his father died. Then his wife, Evgenia, died. Then his student and brother-in-law Fyodor died. Then both of his sons died — all in a short period. [2]

He remarried one of his students, Olga Lagoda, who died giving birth to their daughter. [2]

Through it all, he painted. The forests in his work aren't escape — they're too real, too textured, too present for that word. They're more like witness: something that was there before him and would remain after him, indifferent to human suffering. A pine forest doesn't mourn.

Some contemporaries criticized him for this — called him more of a craftsman than a "real" artist, too focused on botanical accuracy to convey genuine emotion. But that critique misunderstands what Shishkin was doing. He wasn't ignoring emotion. He was finding it in the one place that would never leave him: the Russian woods. [2]

Why He Matters Now

In 2026, we are drowning in AI-generated imagery — technically flawless, emotionally weightless. Shishkin's work is the antidote. Every painting carries the weight of hours spent walking through actual forests, studying actual trees, watching actual light move through actual leaves. He drew from life, not from a latent space.

His tree drawings — Part of the Trunk of a Pine (1890), studies of bark and needles — feel almost scientific in their rigor. They're the work of someone who believed that the truth of a thing was worth the time it took to render it accurately. That belief, applied across a career, produced a body of work that makes you feel the forest — the damp earth, the filtered light, the immense silence of the old woods. [2]

The nickname "Czar of the Forest" is fitting, but it doesn't capture the full picture. Shishkin wasn't a ruler of trees. He was a servant of them. He spent his life trying to do justice to something vast and indifferent and beautiful — and in that attempt, he produced some of the most extraordinary landscape paintings ever made.
