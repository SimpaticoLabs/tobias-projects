The Blue That Was Worth More Than Gold

In 1828, a French chemist named Jean Baptiste Guimet solved a problem that had frustrated painters for centuries: how to make the most beautiful blue in the world without grinding up a semi-precious gemstone that cost more than gold.

The story of blue pigment is a detective story spanning 6,000 years — a tale of accidental chemistry, mineral scarcity, a national prize, and the strange economics of color. Here's how we went from a color worth more than diamonds to one that costs pennies per gram.

The Blue Problem

Blue is surprisingly rare in nature. Unlike reds, yellows, and browns — which are abundant in soils, clays, and organic materials — vibrant blue pigments are geologically scarce. Most blue things in nature (sky, water, butterfly wings) produce their color through structural effects, not pigments you can scrape off and use. [1]

Ancient artists had exactly two decent blue options: Egyptian blue (a synthetic calcium copper silicate developed around 2500 BCE, the first synthetic pigment in history) and azurite (a copper carbonate mineral). Both had problems. Egyptian blue was known to the Romans but its production method was lost after the fall of the Roman Empire. Azurite was plentiful but tended to turn green over time as it weathered. [2]

Then, around the 6th century CE, a new blue entered the picture — one that would become the most expensive pigment the world has ever known.

Lapis Lazuli: The $30,000 Rock

Lapis lazuli is a metamorphic rock containing the mineral lazurite, which produces an intense, pure blue unlike anything else in nature. The only known source for centuries was a single mining complex in the remote Sar-e-Sang valley of Badakhshan province in what is now northeastern Afghanistan. Miners extracted it by setting fires against the rock face and then dousing it with water — the thermal shock caused the rock to fracture — a technique that hadn't changed since antiquity. [3]

The rock was shipped overland through Persia and the Levant, reaching Venice — the primary European port of entry — by the 13th and 14th centuries. The Venetians, who had a near-monopoly on the trade, ground it into pigment, creating ultramarine — from the Latin ultramarinus, meaning "beyond the sea," a name that captured both its exotic origin and its transport cost.

The economics were brutal. In Renaissance Europe, lapis lazuli cost three to five thousand francs per pound (roughly equivalent to $30,000–$50,000 per kilo in today's money). By comparison, synthetic ultramarine would later sell for about 400 francs per pound — a 10x difference. [4]

Artists treated ultramarine as a strategic asset, not a pigment. They used it sparingly and only where it would have maximum impact.

  • Vermeer, known for his masterful use of blue, reserved ultramarine for the most important surfaces in his paintings — the turban in Girl with a Pearl Earring, the milkmaid's apron. He didn't waste it on backgrounds. [5]
  • Perugino painted only the top layer of the Virgin Mary's robes in ultramarine. The underlying layers were painted with the cheaper azurite, and only the visible surface got the expensive stuff. [6]
  • In illuminated manuscripts, ultramarine was so precious that it was often applied as a final, almost transparent wash — a thin veil of luxury over cheaper pigments beneath. [3]

Painters' contracts from the period specify exactly which parts of a painting could use ultramarine — typically only the robes of Christ and the Virgin Mary. Using ultramarine on any other subject was considered a waste. [4]

The Chemistry Prize

By the early 19th century, the pressure to find a synthetic alternative was intense. Artists needed a cool, deep blue — it's a necessary component of any balanced palette — but natural ultramarine was so expensive that many simply did without.

The breakthrough began with an unlikely observer: Johann Wolfgang von Goethe.

Yes, that Goethe — the poet, playwright, and natural philosopher. Around 1787, during his Italian journey, Goethe noticed strange blue deposits on the walls of lime kilns near Palermo, Sicily. The deposits were a glassy blue substance that seemed to form accidentally in the furnace. He noted that it might serve as a substitute for lapis lazuli in decorative applications. He did not, however, suggest grinding it into pigment. [4]

Decades later, samples from the Saint-Gobain glassworks in France caught the attention of chemist Nicolas Vauquelin. In 1814, Vauquelin published a paper showing that these blue furnace deposits were chemically similar to lapis lazuli. The pieces were falling into place.

In 1824, the Société d'Encouragement pour l'Industrie Nationale made an extraordinary offer: a prize of six thousand francs to anyone who could produce a synthetic ultramarine costing no more than three hundred francs per kilogram. [4]

The prize sat unclaimed for four years. Every submission turned out to be an imitation based on cobalt or Prussian blue — not true ultramarine. The Society had set a high bar.

On February 4, 1828, the prize was awarded to Jean Baptiste Guimet, a French chemist and industrialist. Guimet had actually developed his method in 1826 — heating kaolinite (a type of clay), sodium carbonate, and sulfur in a kiln — but kept it secret until he was certain he'd won. The resulting pigment, French ultramarine, was chemically identical to natural lapis lazuli and, by some accounts, even more vivid. [7]

Independently, Christian Gottlob Gmelin, a professor of chemistry at the University of Tübingen, discovered a slightly different method based on Vauquelin's analysis. He published his results just one month after Guimet's award. A bitter rivalry ensued, with Gmelin claiming he'd beaten Guimet to the discovery. France upheld Guimet's right to the prize. By 1830, Guimet had opened a factory in Fleurieu-sur-Saône, and Gmelin's method was being produced at the Meissen porcelain works in Germany. [4]

The price of blue had just collapsed.

Prussian Blue: The Blue That Happened by Accident

But Guimet wasn't the first to crack the blue problem. Over a century earlier, the first modern synthetic blue pigment was created entirely by accident in Berlin.

Around 1704, the German chemist and dye maker Heinrich Diesbach was trying to make a red lake pigment. He was boiling iron sulfate and potassium alkali together — a standard procedure — but the alkali he used had been stored in a barrel that previously held animal blood. The blood had contaminated the alkali with potassium ferrocyanide, an iron-cyanide compound.

The "red" turned an unexpectedly deep, intense blue. [8]

Diesbach had stumbled onto Prussian blue — the first stable, lightfast synthetic blue pigment available since the loss of Egyptian blue's formula. It hit the European pigment market between 1704 and 1710 and was immediately revolutionary. Its deep blue color approached the quality of ultramarine at a much lower price, and it was extremely permanent. [9]

The pigment was named after Prussia — the emerging German kingdom where it was discovered — and quickly spread across Europe. Major artists adopted it:

  • Thomas Gainsborough used it extensively in his landscapes.
  • Canaletto painted the Venetian sky with it — a specific, identifiable sky blue that art historians now recognize as Prussian blue. [9]
  • Hokusai used it in the early 1830s for The Great Wave off Kanagawa — the deeper blues of the massive wave and the distant Mount Fuji are Prussian blue, imported to Japan by Dutch traders. [9]

Interestingly, Prussian blue also found an unusual medical application. It is approved as a treatment for thallium and radioactive cesium poisoning — the pigment binds to these heavy metals in the digestive tract and prevents them from being absorbed into the body. The same substance that colored Hokusai's waves is used to treat radiation exposure. [10]

The Modern Blue Revolution

The 19th and 20th centuries brought a cascade of new blues:

  • Cobalt blue (first described 1777) — a vivid, stable blue used extensively in ceramics. [2]
  • Cerulean blue (developed 1860s) — a sky-blue variant of cobalt stannate.
  • Phthalocyanine blue (first prepared 1927) — "phthalo blue," a synthetic organic pigment with extraordinary tinting strength. Today it's one of the most widely used blue pigments in inks, paints, and plastics. [11]
  • Indigo — technically a dye, now produced synthetically at about 20,000 tonnes per year, mostly for blue jeans. It is, by volume, the dominant blue pigment in the world. [12]

The most recent addition to the blue family is YInMn blue, discovered in 2009 by chemist Mas Subramanian and his graduate student Andrew Smith at Oregon State University. They were experimenting with materials for electronics and mixed manganese oxide with yttrium and indium at high temperatures. The result was a vivid, durable blue — the first new inorganic blue pigment discovered in over 200 years. It was so stable that the paint industry immediately licensed it. [13]

What Blue Teaches Us

The history of blue pigment is a lens on something bigger: the economics of scarcity and the democratizing power of chemistry.

A color that was once worth more than gold — reserved for the robes of the Virgin Mary, hoarded by painters like a precious metal, the subject of a national prize — is now so cheap that it's used to color plastic toys, laundry detergent, and the uniform of the modern world (blue jeans). Every kid with a crayon has access to blues that would have made a Renaissance painter weep.

The 1824 Société prize catalyzed the end of an era. But it's also a reminder: some of the most valuable breakthroughs happen when someone asks "what if we could make the expensive thing cheap?" — and the answer turns out to be "heat some clay and sulfur in a kiln, and good things happen."
