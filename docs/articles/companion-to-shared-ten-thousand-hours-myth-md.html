🧵 The "10,000-hour rule" is the most influential pop-science idea of the 21st century. It's also largely wrong. Here's what the expertise research actually says — and what Malcolm Gladwell got wrong. (thread)

1/ The rule came from a 1993 study of violin students at a Berlin music academy. The top performers had averaged ~10,000 hours of practice by age 20.

Gladwell turned this into: "10,000 hours is the magic number of greatness."

But the study's author, Anders Ericsson, called it "a provocative generalization" — not a rule.

2/ Problem: half of the best violinists in Ericsson's study had NOT reached 10,000 hours by 20. The number was an average, not a threshold. By 18, the same students had ~7,400 hours — so if there's a magic number, why does it keep changing?

3/ The bigger error: Gladwell conflated "doing something" with "deliberate practice." Logging hours at your job ≠ structured training that pushes you past your limits. Ericsson emphasized that mechanical repetition doesn't improve ability — targeted, feedback-rich practice does.

4/ A 2019 meta-analysis found deliberate practice accounts for only: • 26% of performance variance in games • 21% in music • 1% in professional attainment

Other factors (talent, opportunity, timing) dominate — especially at elite levels.

5/ The honest take: deliberate practice is necessary but not sufficient for world-class performance. You won't get there without it, but it alone won't get you there either.

The real lesson: practice the right things, in the right way, with feedback, for as long as it takes — but don't mistake a catchy number for a science of skill.
